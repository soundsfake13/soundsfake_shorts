# Day 28 — Romans, urine and teeth: sources checked this run

- Catullus, poem 39 (Latin text): https://blogs.dickinson.edu/catullus/2015/05/12/teeth-too-bright-catullus-39/ — mocks Egnatius' constant grin; "Nunc Celtiber es: Celtiberia in terra, quod quisque minxit, hoc sibi solet mane dentem atque russam defricare gingivam."
- Smith's Dictionary (1875) "Dentifricium" + B. Thayer notes: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Dentifricium.html — cites Catullus 39, Diodorus 5.33.5, Strabo 3.4.16 on Iberians rinsing teeth with urine.
- S. McDaniel, "Did the Ancient Romans Really Wash Their Teeth with Urine?": https://talesoftimesforgotten.com/2021/03/03/did-the-ancient-romans-really-wash-their-teeth-with-urine/ — Catullus (c. 84–54 BC) presents it as a Celtiberian habit, not Roman; Roman fullers did use stale urine to bleach clothes.
- World History Encyclopedia, "The Fullers of Ancient Rome": https://www.worldhistory.org/article/46/the-fullers-of-ancient-rome/ — fullers used urine as detergent; collected from jars in the streets; Vespasian taxed it.
- Wikipedia, "Fullo": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullo — ammonia derived from urine used in fulling.
- Wikipedia, "Pecunia non olet": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pecunia_non_olet — urine used by launderers as ammonia source to whiten woollen togas; Vespasian's urine tax (~AD 70).
- Suetonius, Life of Vespasian 23.3 (Loeb transl., LacusCurtius): https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/suetonius/12caesars/vespasian*.html — Titus objected to the tax; Vespasian held a coin to his son's nose, asked if the smell offended him; "Yet it comes from urine."

Framing decisions: the video says "some ancient people" and "a habit blamed on ancient Spain" (not "Romans brushed their teeth with urine"), because the sources attribute the tooth-rinsing to Celtiberians and treat it as possibly satire. No claim that urine actually whitens teeth. "Money doesn't stink" is the traditional paraphrase of the anecdote.
